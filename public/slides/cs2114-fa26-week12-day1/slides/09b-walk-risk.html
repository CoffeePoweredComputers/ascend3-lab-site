A: add(int, T) and set(int, T) arrive unchanged from List. One call puts 25 before 7, and the list is out of order.

B: a search on a chain visits nodes one at a time, so it is O(n) whether it is inherited or written from scratch. A chain has no binary search to lose. On an array the order allows binary search, which is in Week 14.

C: remove(int) deletes by position. After remove(2) the list reads 3, 12, 20, and it is still in order.

Answer: A [PL twin sortedLists/sortedlists_inheritance; confirm in Runestone’s results view] — the inherited positional mutators let a client put an entry anywhere, so the list can lose its order. Step 1, A: the same add(2, 25) as on the slide before the vote. The vote’s set(int, T) is the textbook’s replace(givenPosition, newEntry). Both come from List unchanged, and neither compares anything. Step 2, B: put the chain back. contains(12) on a chain starts at firstNode and visits 3, 7 and 12, one node at a time. That is O(n) for an inherited contains and for one written from scratch, so inheriting loses nothing. Binary search needs to jump to the middle entry in one step, which an array allows and a chain does not. Binary search is Week 14’s topic. Step 3, C: remove by position takes an entry out. Here remove(2) removes 7, the entry at position 2. What is left, 3, 12, 20, is still in order, because removing an entry never puts two entries in the wrong order. The textbook’s LinkedSortedList keeps remove(givenPosition) for that reason. Step 4: A is the answer. Next is Part 3, the cost of each design.
