Two inherited methods let a client break the order

A walkthrough under a program-counter bar. The code is two lines of client code on a SortedList that extends LList: sorted dot add of 2, 25, then sorted dot replace of 1, 30. On the right, the sorted chain 3, 7, 12, 20 with firstNode on 3. Step one: the line add of 2, 25 is current. Step two: a node holding 25 grows under the gap between 3 and 7, its next arrow draws on to 7, and 3's next arrow moves to 25; the list reads 3, 25, 7, 12, 20. Step three: a maroon ring appears round 25, which is larger than 7 but comes before it. Step four: the line replace of 1, 30 is current; the caption says that the vote calls this method set. Step five: the 3 in the first node is struck through and replaced by 30; the list reads 30, 25, 7, 12, 20. Step six: a maroon ring appears round 30, and the caption says that neither method reads the order, and that both come from LList unchanged, so a SortedList that extends LList has them.

client code; SortedList extends LList

  1. sorted.add(2, 25);
  2. sorted.replace(1, 30);

SortedList extends LList and changes nothing, so it inherits add(newPosition, newEntry). The client calls add(2, 25).

add(2, 25) puts 25 at position 2, after 3. The list reads 3, 25, 7, 12, 20.

25 is larger than 7 but comes before it. The list is no longer in sorted order.

replace(1, 30) sets the entry at position 1 to 30. The vote calls this method set(int, T).

The entry 3 is replaced by 30. The list reads 30, 25, 7, 12, 20.

Neither method reads the order. Both come from LList unchanged, so a SortedList that extends LList has them.

Suppose SortedList extends LList and adds nothing that blocks the inherited methods. The 11.2 module says such a class inherits two methods that a client can use to destroy the order of a sorted list. Positions in the textbook’s LList start at 1. Step 1: the client calls add(2, 25). Step 2: LList’s add puts 25 at position 2, right after 3, as it would in any list. Step 3: 25 now comes before 7, so the list is not sorted. getPosition, contains and the sorted add itself all assume the order, so their results are now wrong. Step 4: the client calls replace(1, 30). The vote calls this kind of method set(int, T); the textbook’s name is replace(givenPosition, newEntry). Step 5: the entry at position 1, which was 3, is now 30. Step 6: 30 is larger than every entry after it. Neither method compares anything; they put the entry where the client says. Both are inherited from LList unchanged. Next: how the textbook keeps these methods away from clients.
