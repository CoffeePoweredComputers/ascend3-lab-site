Four small diagrams in a two-by-two grid, lettered A to D in orange, each the same picture of getSign: three vertical lanes side by side, headed x greater than 0, x less than 0, and else, and footed 1, minus 1, and 0. Option by option, the test’s inputs appear above the lanes they enter and each entered lane fills blue from the top down; a counter to the right of each diagram reads two of three branches for A, for B and for C, and three of three for D. Last, under D, its third assertion, assertEquals(1, getSign(0)), appears and a maroon cross covers the 0 at the foot of the else lane — the lane ran, and the expectation was wrong. A caption underneath: coverage asks did this line run; it never asks was it right; Project 1 asks for both, a normal case and a bad-input case per method.

Coverage asks “did this line run”. It never asks “was it right”. Project 1 asks for both: a normal case and a bad-input case per method.

Answer: D — 7, minus 2 and 0 are the only set that enters all three branches. Step 1 — A: 5 takes the positive lane, minus 3 the negative one; the else lane never runs. Two of three. Step 2 — B: three assertions, and the room reads that as three cases — but 10 and 2 are both positive, so they land in the same lane and the negative branch never runs. Two of three. Counting assertions is not counting branches. Step 3 — C: minus 9 and 0; the positive lane never runs. Two of three. Step 4 — D: 7, minus 2, 0 — every lane fills, three of three, 100% branch coverage. Step 5 — the sting, worth the whole question: D’s last assertion expects 1 where the method returns 0, so the test fails — and the counter does not move. It still has 100% coverage. Coverage asks “did this line run”, not “is it right”, which is exactly why Project 1’s Deliverable 3 asks for two kinds of JUnit test per method — a normal case and a bad-input case. A line that ran is not a line that was right. Plant: Part 3 — code where every line runs and one still explodes.
