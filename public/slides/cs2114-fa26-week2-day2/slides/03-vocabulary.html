VOCABULARY

Three words we will use all semester

On the left a stick figure, the client, faces a contract sheet titled Bag that lists add, remove, contains and size over a signature line; the sheet draws itself first and is labelled Interface, written down. A tall stone wall stands to the right of the sheet. Behind the wall a row of six array slots grows, one of them holding an item, labelled Data structure, how. Beneath it a chain of three linked nodes grows, arrow by arrow, ending in a null slash: a second way to keep the same promise. Then the wall is labelled Abstract data type, what, and a short arrow from the client to the sheet shows that the client only ever sees the sheet. Last the caption: a Bag is an ADT; ArrayBag keeps the promise with an array; your Project 1 spec asks both: the promise, and how you keep it.

Step 0: a client at left, a stone wall to the right of where the sheet will be. Say nothing about the wall yet. Step 1 — the sheet draws on: a contract titled Bag with four lines, add, remove, contains, size, and a signature line. This is the interface: the promise, written down in Java. A class that implements it has signed the contract. Step 2 — behind the wall an array of slots grows, one slot holding an item: a data structure, the how. A concrete way of holding the collection in memory. Step 3 — a chain of linked nodes grows under it: a second way to keep the same promise. Point at both and say the client on the left cannot tell which one is back there, and does not need to. Step 4 — the wall gets its name: abstract data type, the what. The arrow stops at the sheet: the client only ever sees the sheet. Land the last line slowly: it is the whole point of the course. Two classes can implement the same interface, behave identically from the outside, and differ completely inside — that is the difference students will be asked about on every test this semester. Step 5 — the caption: a Bag is an ADT; ArrayBag keeps the promise with an array, and it is not the only class that could. Project 1’s specification asks exactly these two questions of every class you design: what does it promise — the method signatures — and how does it keep the promise — which structure holds the data, and why that one. That is where we go next.
