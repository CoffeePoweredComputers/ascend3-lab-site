The Eight Key Questions from the JMU handbook as a board of eight cards in two rows, each with its name and the handbook’s question: Fairness, what is the fair or just thing to do; Outcomes, what are the short- and long-term outcomes for all involved; Responsibilities, what duties or obligations apply; Character, what actions will help me to become my ideal self; Liberty, how does respect for freedom or personal autonomy apply; Empathy, how would I respond if I cared about those involved; Authority, what do legitimate authorities expect; Rights, what rights apply. Press by press, four cards light up in orange and take an option letter, with a sentence under the board about the deny list: D, Fairness, blocking a whole country locks out millions of people for what a botnet on some of their machines did; E, Outcomes, the service is back up in the short term, but real users on those addresses are locked out for good; A, Responsibilities, the team owes its users a working service, including the users behind a blocked address; C, Liberty, someone whose laptop was in the botnet without their knowledge loses access and nobody asked them. On the fifth press a dashed grey chip appears beside the sentence, B, Monetary gains/losses, with no card: money is not one of the eight; it can be one outcome among others, but it cannot tell you whether the block is fair. Last, the five options return in place of the sentence, A, C, D and E struck through and B left standing.

Answer: B — Monetary gains/losses [key ASSUMED; confirm in Runestone]. These are the eight questions from the JMU handbook in your 6.1 module, with the handbook’s own question for each. Four of the five options are on the board; we ask each one about the deny list. Step 1 — D, Fairness: a deny list can hold a whole country. Blocking it locks out millions of people for what a botnet running on some of their machines did. Step 2 — E, Outcomes: the short-term outcome is good, the attack stops and the service comes back. The long-term outcome of a permanent block is that legitimate users on those addresses never get in again. Step 3 — A, Responsibilities: the team has a duty to keep the service running, and the users behind a blocked address are users it owes that to as well. Step 4 — C, Liberty: a botnet runs on computers whose owners do not know. That person loses access they did nothing to give up, and nobody asked them. Step 5 — B has no card. Money can enter as one outcome among others, what the outage costs, what the block costs, but it is not one of the eight, and on its own it cannot tell you whether the block is fair or who it hurts. That makes it the least helpful. Step 6: B. Next: Vote 2, the other tool from 6.1, the ACM Code.
