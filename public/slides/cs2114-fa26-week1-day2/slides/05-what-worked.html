What worked: Homework/Challenge Exercises

The same project clock runs along the bottom, from released to due, above a thin footing line: a building site and a deadline. Three habits build in turn. Start early — the marker appears at released and the orange time-you-have bar opens all the way to due. Understand before you code — on the left a blueprint titled spec plus UML draws itself, three dashed courses of a wall, while a maroon used-time segment creeps to a fifth of the bar. Build incrementally — on the right a stone wall rises course by course, each checked before the next, used time reaching sixty percent. A bracket over the orange remainder reads Web-CAT runs, question time: what starting early bought.

Cover the figure and "What worked" is three titles; the drawing carries the argument. Step 1 (01 Start early): the marker appears at "released" — where the last slide's marker was before it slid late — and the whole orange span of time you have opens to "due". Projects take longer than they look. An early start buys you Web-CAT iterations, and time to ask. Step 2 (02 Understand before you code): the blueprint is drawn before any stone. Read the whole spec. Study the UML. A misread design cascades into every class you write. Say "the plan is the wall" when the courses match. The maroon "time used" creeps to about a fifth: reading costs time, and it is time well spent. Step 3 (03 Build incrementally): the three courses go up one after another, each checked before the next. Class by class, testing each piece before the next. It keeps the bugs small and findable. Say once aloud that the check on every course is "tests pass". Step 4: the orange that remains gets its name — Web-CAT runs, time to ask. This is what step 1 bought; on the last slide the same orange was a stub. Say that three courses is a shape, not a count of classes, and that the time-used ratio is illustrative, not measured.
