A — Sarsa, Denny, Hellas & Leinonen, ICER 2022 (literature/sarsa.pdf), DOI 10.1145/3501385.3543957. 240 exercises generated with OpenAI Codex by priming with a single worked exercise plus keywords; a random half (n=120) was hand-audited. Verbatim: “75.0% were sensible, 81.8% were novel, and 76.7% had a matching sample solution.” The machine-checkable part was the weak link — 84.6% (203/240) had a sample solution, 89.7% (182/203) of those ran, 170/240 had automated tests, but of the 165 with both, only 51 (30.9%) had a solution that passed its own generated tests. Most failures weren’t logic: the solution printed where the test expected a return. Note the deck previously carried this as a full priming→completion exhibit (slides/our-fields-timeline.html, still on disk, now out of the manifest) — pull it up if anyone wants to see an actual prompt.

B — Fan, Zhang, Paquette & Zhang, Findings of EMNLP 2023, pp. 7406–7421 (literature/Fan2023-LLMCodeTracingQuestions.pdf). 176 code-snippet/question pairs, 158 from CSAwesome (Runestone, AP CS A–aligned Java) plus 18 from YouTube; four CS graduate students each rated 44 randomly selected snippets. Verbatim: “Approximately 56% (99 out of 176) of GPT4-generated questions were mistakenly identified by experts as human-generated, and about 20% (35 out of 176) of human-authored questions were misattributed to the GPT4.” The authors read the asymmetry as evaluator bias toward crediting quality to humans. The counter-intuitive bit is worth saying: “Contrary to our expectations, the experiment showed that the few-shot method introduced a significant bias towards the example questions, thus narrowing the diversity in the generated questions. Consequently, we opted for the zero-shot generation.” More prompt engineering made it worse. On the rating criteria humans still won narrowly on three of four (relevance to LO, clarity, difficulty appropriateness); relevance to the snippet showed no significant difference.

Cut from this slide: Refute questions — Agarwal, Kumar, Raman & Karkare, CompEd 2023, DOI 10.1145/3576882.3617916. Dropped because no model generates anything in it, so it does not belong on a slide about generation methods. A Refute question “asks students for a counter-example which proves that a given code fragment is an incorrect solution for a given task,” and the items come from data, not a model: from 6 code-writing questions in a Fall 2015 C course their autograder had logged 13,847 executable student snapshots, of which 10,574 were buggy; the contribution is an automated filter over that pool, evaluated by surveying 56 experienced instructors, tutors and TAs, then seeded into a public repository via CodeCheck. The LLM is the motivation, not the generator: refute skills “assume greater significance with the emergence of generative AI technologies capable of writing code that is plausible (at least to novice programmers) but not always correct.” Keep this in the pocket — it is the right answer if someone asks why refute questions are missing, and the right slide for it would be one about mining student data rather than generating items. Caveat on the source: not in literature/ — verified from the publisher abstract via Crossref/Semantic Scholar, never from full text.

D — Pădurean, Denny & Singla, BugSpotter, SIGCSE TS 2025; arXiv:2411.14303v2 (literature/Padurean2025-BugSpotterDebuggingExercises.pdf). Two stages, verbatim: “(a) code generation using LLMs and (b) validation of the exercise through execution.” Four checks against the problem’s existing test suite: discard if the fixed code C_F or buggy code C_B fails to compile; C_F must solve the problem against the full suite; C_B “should fail at least one of the test cases in the test suite, either by raising a runtime error or by producing an output different from the expected one”; discard anything over a time limit. Yield: 10 candidate tuples per problem at temperature 0.7, SuccOf10 aggregate means 5.33 (stderr 0.7) GPT-4o and 6.11 (stderr 1.1) GPT-3.5 — GPT-3.5 scored higher, which the authors read as evidence it can power the system cheaply without loss of quality. Deployment: “a laboratory task in a large introductory C programming course involving 741 students, taught at the University of Auckland.” Comparison to instructor-written exercises: “all p-values exceed 0.05, with the lowest p-value being 0.065, indicating no significant difference between the two sources.” Accuracy guard: that is a null result — no detected difference, not demonstrated equivalence; the paper reports no equivalence test. Students interact by writing failing test cases, so the exercise doubles as a testing exercise.

The line to land as C reveals. Three generation methods, and only one of them was ever tried on students. Sarsa: the authors’ own audit. Fan: four expert raters. Only BugSpotter put the generated items in front of students — 741 of them — and measured what happened. Say it as a null result, not equivalence: no detected difference from the instructor’s exercises, and the paper ran no equivalence test. That is the handoff to Tan — and note the irony it sets up: in CS the cheapest possible check (run the tests) is sitting right there, which makes “only 10 of 60 studies mention validity” a story about negligence rather than difficulty.
