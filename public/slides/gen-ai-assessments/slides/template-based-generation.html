Key takeaway

Templating item generation approaches front load the work to give a bank of similar questions testing the same construct

Source. Gierl, Lai & Turner 2012, Medical Education 46(8) 757–765, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2012.04289.x. Not in literature/ — verified against the open Medical Council of Canada copy (mcc.ca). Companion: Gierl & Lai 2013, EM:IP 32(3) 36–50, free at ncme.org.

The numbers, verbatim. “From a single item model, 1248 unique items were generated for our Postoperative Fever example.” The figure appears three times in the paper (abstract, Results Stage 3, Discussion). Timing, verbatim: “The 1248 items generated in this example required 6 hours to produce across our three-stage method: Stage 1 (3 hours); Stage 2 (2 hours), and Stage 3 (1 hour).” Do not convert that to an items-per-hour rate — the paper states no such rate, and the derived figure does not survive checking.

What on this slide is drawn from the paper, and what is not. From the paper: the three stage names and their order (the 2012 paper puts the cognitive model first), the timings 3 h / 2 h / 1 h, and 1,248. The bars encode only those three timings against a shared baseline — they are the reason “6 hours” decomposes rather than a claim about volume. The item model in Stage 2 is schematic and ours. The paper’s worked example is a Postoperative Fever model, but we do not hold its element lists, so the five bracketed slots and the wording were written here to match the paper’s own definition — a stem, elements with legal values, constraints between them. Say “schematic” if asked. Deliberately not drawn: any before/after pruning ratio. The paper reports the 1,248 that survived and no count of the combinations IGOR generated first, so a funnel would be an invented figure.

IGOR. Item GeneratOR, a JAVA program. Verbatim: “IGOR is an ‘iterator’, meaning that it contains algorithms for combining elements, subject to the logical constraints in the item model, to generate items. Given item model input, IGOR first generates a set of all possible items. Then, IGOR checks the constraints for each item and removes all illogical element combinations.” Enumerate and prune — pure combinatorics plus constraint filtering. This is the sharpest available contrast with the LLM era: there is no statistical or neural model anywhere in the pipeline. Gierl & Lai frame it as a combinatorial problem that “any linear programming method can be used to solve.”

Say “generated,” never “validated.” No psychometric field data were collected in this paper, and the companion module warns: “the psychometric properties (e.g., item difficulty) of these items must still be evaluated. Item quality is typically determined through a field or pilot testing process.” Scale for reference: the 2013 module reports 256 items from a 1-layer surgery model and 16,384 from an n-layer model — quote those only with the words “items were generated,” since the module’s own self-test reports different (unconstrained) figures for the same two models.

Two traps if questioned. (1) Gierl publishes two conflicting step orderings — the 2013 EM:IP module puts the item model first, the 2012 Medical Education paper puts the cognitive model first — so avoid asserting one canonical arrow. The cognitive model belongs to strong-theory AIG only; weak theory doesn’t use one (weak/strong theory is Drasgow, Luecht & Bennett 2006). (2) “Sibling” is Gierl & Lai’s 2013 term, not the 2012 paper’s. The field’s own pejorative for 1-layer output is worth a laugh: isomorphs “are referred to pejoratively by many test development specialists as ‘clones,’ ‘ghost’ items or ‘Franken-items.’”

The economic motive (second-hand — Rudner, cited in Gierl): a single high-stakes licensure item costs US$1500–2000 to write by hand, so a ~2,000-item bank runs US$3–4 million. That is why anyone built this. Counterpoint for later: template AIG frequently fails its own isomorphism premise — Fu et al. 2022 (EM:IP) found only 9 of 23 templates produced psychometrically equivalent instances without revision.
