Source. Bouvier, Cipriano, Glassey, Petrovska, Anderson, Birillo et al., “The Rest of the Robots: Generative AI in Post-introductory Computing Education,” ITiCSE-WGR 2025, Nijmegen, DOI 10.1145/3760545.3783970 — literature/wg/3760545.3783970.pdf, §8.5 Table 28. Verbatim framing: “Table 28 shows the various changes to assessment practice instructors are considering based on their responses to question 13 of the survey.” Note the word considering — these are reported intentions and practices, not verified implementations.

The denominator, and its bias — say this first if challenged. The survey drew 118 valid responses; “56 out of 118 instructors (47%) reported already integrating (IN) GenAI in their courses.” Table 28 covers only those 56, and is multi-select (the counts sum well above 56). So this is a sample of instructors who chose to bring GenAI into their courses — self-selected away from the exam-hardening camp by construction. It is the right population for “what do integrators do,” and the wrong population for “what is the field doing.” The report says so itself: “Because responses came from a convenience (non-probability) sample and we counted themes from coded open-ended answers, the numbers are indicative instead of population estimates.”

The other working group points the other way — do not hide this. Prather et al., “Beyond the Hype,” ITiCSE-WGR 2024 (literature/wg/3689187.3709614-1.pdf), §5.2.1, thematically coded 63 open-ended responses and found the opposite drift: “(2) Invigilated exams. Thirteen participants reported placing more emphasis on the proctoring of assessments.” and “(3) Increased weighting on exams. Ten participants decreased the weight of unproctored assessments and increased the weight of proctored exams in assigning grades.” But the two only look opposed until you compare the instruments, and the gap is mostly artifact. Ranked by size: (1) Prather’s question was pure free text, so exams had to be written in and were; this one offered a nine-item menu with no exam option, so most answers landed on the menu instead. (2) That menu encodes the 2025 group’s own priors — every option is a process, reflection or task-design move. (3) Prather spans all computing courses; this one is upper-division only, where written proctored exams are a poorer fit than in CS1. (4) Prather files oral exams inside “invigilated exams” (7 of the 13); this survey lists orals separately. Compare like with like — Prather’s free text produced 13 + 10 exam mentions from 63 responses, this survey’s free-text write-ins produced 5 from 56 — and the field has drifted, not reversed.

THE CHART SHOWS ALL NINE OFFERED OPTIONS — and this is the point to have ready. Question 13 was select-all-that-apply from a fixed menu (Appendix C, Table 31). The nine options, verbatim: (1) No Change; (2) Give more points to the process or development journey; (3) Give more attention to validation of results (e.g., evidence of testing); (4) Give more attention to student reflection; (5) Add an oral explanation of the deliverables; (6) Ask for in-person feature changes (e.g., add a new feature to the delivered code); (7) Design tasks that are harder for GenAI to solve; (8) Emphasize responsible GenAI usage over GenAI-proofing; (9) Use version control to monitor progress; (10) Other. No exam option was offered at all — not proctored, not paper, not no-Internet.

Rows left off the chart, and why. Every remaining row in Table 28 arrived through the free-text “Other” box: paper-based exams, labs and tests 3; unsure about changes 2; use proctored exams 1; no-Internet exams 1; more open-ended projects 1; create videos of their work 1; less weight in the grade for GenAI things 1. Note the perfect separation — every menu option scored 4 or more, every write-in scored 3 or fewer. That is the signature of checkbox-versus-write-in, not a finding about what instructors do. So “proctored exams = 1” means one person typed it into a box when it was not on the menu. It does not mean one instructor out of 56 proctors exams. Do not put that number on a slide, and do not let anyone else use it as one.

These are changes made OR planned — the question fuses them. Q13 verbatim: “How have you changed (or plan to change) your approach to assessments in higher-level courses that incorporate GenAI?” The report’s own gloss is “changes to assessment practice instructors are considering.” So none of these counts is behaviour; they are behaviour plus intention, unseparated. Calibration from the same survey: Q10 asked about revising learning objectives and did force the distinction — Table 29 reports Yes 8, “Not yet, but I’m planning” 21, No 26, N/A 1, of the same 56. Intent outran action roughly 2.6 to 1 there. Treat that as a warning about magnitude, not as a conversion factor. Contrast with the previous slide: Prather’s ES-22 asked only about changes “you have made,” and quarantined intent in its own theme (nine had made no changes, six more had plans). Prather measures behaviour; this slide does not.

The reframe, if you need to say it out loud. An oral explanation of the deliverables is an invigilated assessment. So is asking for an in-person feature change. Add those two rows and 41 of the selections are some form of “sit with the student and watch them do it” — the same diagnosis this talk makes, reached independently. The disagreement is not about whether the artifact stopped carrying the signal. It is about what you can afford to do about it at 400 students.

Best supporting artifact — Bouvier §8.5, respondent R52, who arrived at this talk’s position on their own: “I stopped grading for correctness or giving much credit for them [projects]. Instead, students complete on their own (no autograder) and turn in only for completeness. Second, I created ‘Project Evaluations’ which is a paper, in person, proctored assessment to test their understanding of the project. This allows students to use Gen AI tools and each other as much or as little as they’d like.” Unproctored work, proctored evidence.

Pre-empt the anxiety objection. Prather et al. §4.1, in the working group’s own voice: instructors were “concerned about increasing anxiety among students by focusing too heavily on exams, which is a well-known issue.” The answer is the frequent-testing slide earlier in this deck — more, smaller exams lowered reported test anxiety and perceived difficulty. Point back at it rather than arguing.

Arithmetic warning, corrected. §8.6 prints “majority of the integrators (26 of 56; 84%) have not changed their objectives.” The 84% is right — it is 47/56, the 26 “No” plus the 21 “Not yet, but I’m planning,” both of which have indeed not changed. Only the “26 of 56” is mis-transcribed. Do not accuse the authors of an arithmetic error here; the one real inconsistency is §8.2.1’s “𝑛 = 17” against Table 18’s 34. Quote Table 28’s raw counts and do not derive percentages from them.
