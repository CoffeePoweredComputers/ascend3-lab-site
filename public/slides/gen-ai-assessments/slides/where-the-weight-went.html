Key takeaway

The two most common changes were both about putting the assessment back in a room with an invigilator.

Source. Prather, Leinonen, Gorson Benario, Norouzi, Porter et al., “Beyond the Hype: A Comprehensive Review of Current Trends in Generative AI Research, Teaching Practices, and Tools,” ITiCSE-WGR 2024, Milan, DOI 10.1145/3689187.3709614 — literature/wg/3689187.3709614-1.pdf, §5.2.1. Survey question ES-22, verbatim: “Please describe any changes you have made to your assessment approaches in courses you are teaching as a result of GenAI tools.” Method, verbatim: “two members of the working group performed a thematic analysis of the 63 open-ended responses to question ES-22 and any responses to question ES-21 that related to changes in assessments.”

The two exam themes, verbatim. “(2) Invigilated exams. Thirteen participants reported placing more emphasis on the proctoring of assessments. Six described more emphasis on proctoring, for example, ‘programming assignments are now preparation for the proctored weekly quizzes.’ Three participants spoke of the importance of giving exams on paper. Seven spoke of adding or placing more emphasis on oral exams: ‘Oral exams are now a vital part of student assessment.’” And “(3) Increased weighting on exams. Ten participants decreased the weight of unproctored assessments and increased the weight of proctored exams in assigning grades. One participant wrote, ‘Minimized unsupervised assessment weight (e. g., assignments and projects) ... Increased supervised assessment weight (e. g., tests and exams).’ One participant described instituting ‘a minimum cutoff for exams’.” Note the sub-counts overlap — 6 + 3 + 7 = 16 against 13 participants — so do not present them as a partition.

The remaining four themes, for completeness: (4) No change (yet) — “Nine participants said they had not made any changes with an additional six participants saying they had not made changes yet but had plans to in the future.” (1) Process over product — six participants, e.g. “shifting things to watching the process (ongoing use of repos, iteration, trackable code growth, etc.) vs final artifact evaluation.” (5) Confuse the LLM — five, e.g. “Homework assignments have been customized so that LLMs can’t solve them well.” (6) Describe AI use — three, who require students to submit their prompts or chat logs. Nothing is omitted from the chart; all six themes are shown.

The working group asserts it in its own voice, not merely as reported opinion. §6 Discussion: “Lastly, recognizing that LLMs can solve take-home assignments, faculty are allocating more course grades on proctored assessments and creating more, or new, proctored assignments.” And §8.2 Conclusions: “GenAI changes assessments: Educators are increasing the weighting of exams (ES-22), exploring alternative invigilated exam techniques (including oral exams), and are emphasizing assessing the process of learning over correctness of answers.” Worth saying aloud that this conclusion pairs exam-weighting with process assessment — this deck’s two halves, stated by the working group as a single finding.

Denominator is unclear — quote raw counts, never percentages. The report introduces ES-22 as “Yet another follow-up question for educators integrating GenAI,” but only 27 of 76 educators (35.5%) are integrators, and 63 responses are reported. The 63 pools ES-22 with the ES-21 responses that touched assessment. The population therefore cannot be pinned down, so any percentage would be invented.

How this reconciles with the next slide — have this ready. The two surveys agree far more than the raw numbers suggest, and the gap is mostly categorisation. Prather counts oral exams inside “invigilated exams” (7 of the 13). Bouvier lists “Add an oral explanation of the deliverables” (28) as a line item separate from “Use proctored exams” (1). So Bouvier’s 1 means written proctored exams specifically — not “nobody moved to invigilated assessment.” Do not reach for a sampling explanation: both surveys aimed this question at integrating educators, so “Prather asked more broadly” does not hold up.

The two charts are not on a common scale and must not be read against each other. These counts come from thematic coding of free text; Bouvier’s come from a multi-select checkbox list. Open-ended coding systematically produces lower counts, because people do not spontaneously write down everything they would tick. Bouvier’s selections average roughly 2.6 per respondent; these themes average well under one per response. The difference in magnitude is an instrument artifact, not a change in the field.
