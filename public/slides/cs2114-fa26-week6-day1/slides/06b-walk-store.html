VOTE 2 · THE ANSWER

Shared fields move up; each subclass keeps its own

A class diagram that builds. First, three class boxes in a row, Shoe, Shirt and WaterBottle, each listing its fields, and each starting with productID and price. Then a Product box grows in above them, the productID and price lines travel up out of all three boxes into it, the three boxes shrink to their own fields, and generalisation arrows draw from the three up to Product. Then a two-line caption tests the other options: C fails the is-a test, a Shoe is not a Shirt; B writes the shared fields twice; D three times; E, an interface promises methods and holds no fields. Last, a verdict strip: A is right, B to E struck through.

  1. A · one Product superclass
  2. B · Clothing beside Product
  3. C · Shoe extends Shirt
  4. D · no inheritance
  5. E · interface only
Answer: A. Step 1: write the three classes as they are described and look at the top of each field list — productID and price, three times. Step 2: that repetition is the signal. A field every class repeats belongs one level up: Product holds productID and price, the three become subclasses, and each keeps only what is its own. Shoe still has size, brand and tractionType; it has not lost anything; it has stopped repeating two fields. Step 3: the others. C: inheritance means is-a, and a Shoe is not a Shirt, even if both have a size. B: Clothing is a fine idea, but the option puts it beside Product, not under it, so productID and price get written in Clothing and again in Product. D writes them three times. E: an interface can promise getPrice(), but it cannot hold the price; every class would still declare the field. Step 4: A. The takeaway: a hierarchy is a design decision you can defend with two tests — is-a, and where each field is written. Next: what counts as a requirement.
