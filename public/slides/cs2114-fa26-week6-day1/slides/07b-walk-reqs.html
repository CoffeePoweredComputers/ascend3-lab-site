What it does, and how well it does it

Two dashed bins, labelled what the system does on the left and how well it does it on the right, with the five shall statements as cards in a column between them. One card per press slides into a bin and picks up a tag: A, available 24 hours, to the right, availability; B, AES-256 passwords, right, security; C, 95 percent of searches within 2 seconds, right, performance; D, apply a discount code at checkout, to the left, behaviour; E, 10,000 concurrent users, right, capacity. Then a caption: non-functional requirements are where values get written down, who the system protects, who it serves; and a verdict strip with D right and A, B, C, E struck through.

  1. D · apply a discount code
  2. A · available 24/7
  3. B · AES-256 passwords
  4. C · 95% within 2 s
  5. E · 10,000 users
Answer: D. Two bins: what the system does, and how well it does it. Step 1: A, available 24/7 — that is not a thing it does, it is a promise about how well: availability, right bin. Step 2: B, AES-256 for passwords — a promise about how safely: security, right. Step 3: C, 95% of searches within 2 seconds — how fast: performance, right. Step 4: D, apply a discount code at checkout — something a user can do and the system does; a functional requirement, the only one, left bin. Step 5: E, 10,000 concurrent users — how big: capacity, right. Step 6: look at the right-hand bin. Availability, security, performance, capacity: none of them is a feature, and every one of them is a value written down as "shall" — who the system protects, who it serves, how it is allowed to fail. The functional bin says what you build; the non-functional bin says what kind of system you are building. Part 3 gives you words for arguing about those. Next: ethics, part two.
