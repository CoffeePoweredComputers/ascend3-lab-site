FIRST, A QUESTION FOR YOU

Think back over every class you have ever sat in.

A row of twelve blank class cards runs across the top, labelled ninth grade at the left end and last semester at the right — every class they have taken. Below it, two questions arrive in turn. First, at the left: which one was the worst? The words students give for that class appear in grey: boring, no interaction, no help, no feedback, too hard, uncaring. Then, at the right: and the best? Its words appear in orange: active, immediate feedback, deliberate practice, social, fun, caring.

Which one was the worst?

  • boring
  • no interaction
  • no help
  • no feedback
  • too hard
  • uncaring

And the best?

  • active
  • immediate feedback
  • deliberate practice
  • social, fun, caring
Let the strip sit for a beat — that is every class since ninth grade. Step 1 asks for the worst: take three or four answers from the room before step 2 puts the words up. Step 3 asks for the best; again three or four answers, then step 4. Then say the point of the pair out loud: nobody ever names “a very clear lecture” as their best learning experience — the best one is always active, social and quick to tell you when you are wrong. Peer instruction is the cheapest way to buy that whole second row at once, and the next slide draws what it looks like.
