Lecture vs Peer Instruction: Who is thinking?

Two rooms drawn side by side, titled Lecture and Peer instruction. On the left, the room everyone already knows: twelve students seated in rows, every head facing front, and one figure at the front in orange — the only person in the picture doing anything. It is captioned one person thinking. Then the second room arrives on the right: the same twelve students, now seated in pairs with each head turned toward a partner, hands go up across the room in orange one after another, and the figure who was at the front is now standing in the aisle. It is captioned everyone thinking.

Ask before stepping: which of these two rooms have you spent more hours in? Step 1 — the same twelve people, the same hour, the same instructor; the only difference is who is doing the work, and notice the front of the room is empty. Step 2 — roughly twice the learning gains in the physics studies, about half a letter grade in computer science. Two things students who took this course said, worth reading out here: “It really makes you realise exactly what mistakes you are making — and you don’t feel as bad about being wrong when you can see classmates thinking the same way.” And: “Sometimes when you get lost in other classes, you are lost for the rest of the lecture. The questions make you notice you are confused before it is too late.” Both say the same thing from two directions: the vote is a cheap way to find out you are wrong while there is still time. The alternative is finding out on the project, in Week 5.
