Whichever path you took, you have to convince readers the analysis is trustworthy. The
*criteria differ by tradition* — don't borrow the wrong vocabulary.

## Two vocabularies for rigor

- **Codebook / positivist:** reliability and validity — including IRR (Lesson 5) and a
  clear, auditable procedure.
- **Interpretivist / reflexive:** **trustworthiness** — credibility, transferability,
  dependability, confirmability <Cite id="lincoln-guba-1985" /> — plus reflexivity and
  quality criteria for the method <Cite id="braun-clarke-2021-onesize" />.

Common techniques (use the ones that fit your stance):

- **Audit trail** — dated memos and decisions, so others can follow your reasoning <Cite id="lincoln-guba-1985" />.
- **Triangulation** — multiple data sources, analysts, or methods <Cite id="denzin-2012" />.
- **Member checking** — returning findings to participants. Useful, but not a simple
  "validation" stamp — treat it critically <Cite id="birt-2016" />.

## How much data is "enough"?

The traditional answer is **saturation** — collect until new data stops yielding new
codes/themes. <Cite id="guest-2006" bare /> found saturation within the first ~12
interviews in their study. But the concept is slippery: people mean different things by it
<Cite id="saunders-2018" />.

Two important refinements:

- **Information power** <Cite id="malterud-2016" />: the more relevant information your
  sample holds (narrow aim, dense dialogue, strong theory), the *fewer* participants you
  need — a more defensible logic than a fixed count.
- In reflexive TA, saturation can be the *wrong* frame entirely, because meaning is
  generated by the analyst, not "found" and exhausted <Cite id="braun-clarke-2021-saturate" />.

<Callout type="tip" title="State your rationale, not a magic number">
"We interviewed until saturation" is weak on its own. Say which kind of saturation (or
information power), how you judged it, and why it fits your design.
</Callout>
