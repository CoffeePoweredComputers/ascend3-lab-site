This is the **codebook / coding-reliability** path. The goal: a shared, well-defined
codebook that *different people* can apply consistently <Cite id="hsieh-shannon-2005" />
<Cite id="gale-2013" />.

## How the codebook gets built

The codebook doesn't arrive fully formed — and it isn't one person's draft handed to the
team. It's built **collaboratively from the first cycle**: two coders develop in parallel, then
reconcile. Here's the whole loop at a glance (the sections after it zoom in):

<Steps>
1. **Assemble a starter subset.** Pick a few transcripts that look maximally different, so early codes have to stretch to fit <Cite id="guest-2006" />.
2. **Agree on the coding unit.** Together, decide what a "chunk" is — a line, a sentence, or a *meaning unit* — and apply it consistently across coders <Cite id="krippendorff-2018" />.
3. **Each coder open-codes the subset, independently.** Both work the *same* transcripts alone, generating first-cycle codes inductively from the data and/or deductively from a framework or research question (the [reasoning stances](/wiki/qualitative/initial-coding) from the previous lesson) — producing *two* parallel draft codebooks, which guards against anchoring on one person's view <Cite id="thomas-2006" /> <Cite id="hsieh-shannon-2005" />.
4. **Reconcile into one shared codebook.** Compare the two drafts and consolidate them; give each surviving code a name, definition, anchor example, and apply / don't-apply rules — the structured shape shown in **Write the codebook** below <Cite id="guest-2006" />.
5. **Apply the shared codebook to the next set.** Where codes don't fit, add, split, or merge them (the **Consolidate redundant codes** step below) — the codebook is a living document, not a fixed list.
6. **Version and log.** Bump the codebook version and record every change in your audit trail, then repeat on new transcripts until it stabilizes (constant comparison) <Cite id="saldana-2021" /> <Cite id="gale-2013" />.
</Steps>

The rest of this lesson zooms into the three moves that matter most: consolidating
redundant codes, writing transferable entries, and reconciling across coders.

<Callout type="warning" title="Consensus is not the same as a reliability statistic">
The loop above builds a codebook by **negotiated consensus** — its rigor is the agreement plus
a documented audit trail. That is *not* the same as a reported **κ/α**: the moment your two
coders consolidate, they are no longer independent, so you cannot compute a clean coefficient on
data you've already reconciled. If a reviewer or funder expects a reliability number, hold out a
fresh set and code it *independently first*, then measure agreement (the next lesson) before you
reconcile <Cite id="oconnor-joffe-2020" />.
</Callout>

## Code independently, then reconcile

Once you have a working codebook, the team-coding loop **tests and refines it on fresh
transcripts** — distinct from building it above, and now iterative <Cite id="saldana-2021" />:

<Steps>
1. **Code independently.** Two+ coders apply the codebook to the same transcripts, alone.
2. **Compare.** Find where you agree and where you don't.
3. **Negotiate.** Discuss disagreements; the fix is usually a sharper *definition*, not a winner.
4. **Revise the codebook** and log the decision in your audit trail.
5. **Re-code** with the revised codebook; repeat on new transcripts until stable.
</Steps>

<Callout type="warning" title="Disagreement is information, not failure">
A disagreement usually means the codebook is ambiguous. Resolving it by refining the
definition improves every future coding decision. Averaging codes or deferring to the
senior coder just hides the problem <Cite id="oconnor-joffe-2020" />.
</Callout>
