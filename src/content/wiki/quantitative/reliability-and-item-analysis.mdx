**Reliability** is consistency: how much of the score is signal (true score) rather
than noise. It caps everything downstream — an unreliable outcome measure attenuates
every effect you try to estimate — and it is *necessary but not sufficient*: a
perfectly consistent score can consistently measure the wrong thing.

## The flavors

- **Test–retest** — same students, same test, two occasions.
- **Alternate forms** — two versions built to the same blueprint.
- **Internal consistency** — do the items hang together *within* one sitting? This is
  the one you'll compute weekly, so it gets the scrutiny below.

## Alpha, omega, and what they don't say

**Cronbach's α** is everywhere, and misread almost as often as it's reported
<Cite id="sijtsma-2009" />:

- α assumes every item measures the construct equally well (tau-equivalence) —
  usually false, and then α *underestimates* reliability.
- **High α does not mean unidimensional.** A two-construct item pile can produce a
  lovely α. Dimensionality is checked with factor tools, never inferred from α.
- **α-chasing** — deleting items to maximize "α if deleted" — capitalizes on chance
  and narrows the construct. Don't tune the instrument to one sample's noise.

**McDonald's ω**, computed from factor loadings, drops the equal-items assumption and
is the modern default; report both while reviewers transition <Cite id="mcneish-2018" />.
Two useful companions: **Spearman–Brown** predicts how reliability changes with test
length (the honest answer to "can we shorten the survey?"), and the **standard error
of measurement**, SEM = SD·√(1−reliability), turns reliability into the ± band around
each student's score — the number that makes "α = .85" mean something.

```r
library(psych)
dci <- read.csv("dci-pilot.csv")[, -1]   # drop id
alpha(dci)          # alpha, item-total correlations, alpha-if-deleted
omega(dci)          # omega from a factor model
```

## Classical item analysis: find the broken items

For each item: **difficulty p** (proportion correct — really *easiness*: p = .95
means nearly everyone got it), and **discrimination** — the corrected item–total
correlation (does this item separate strong from weak students?). Healthy knowledge
items usually live around p ≈ .3–.8 with corrected r ≥ .2.

<Reveal summary="Which two — and what's wrong with each?">
**i07** has a *negative* item–total correlation (−.32): stronger students get it
*wrong* more often — the classic signature of a **mis-keyed answer** (the scoring key
marks the wrong option correct). **i12** sits at p = .95 with near-zero
discrimination: a **ceiling item** almost everyone answers correctly, which measures
nothing about this population. The fix for i07 is a key check, not deletion; i12
either gets harder or earns its keep as a warm-up item on an easier population.
</Reveal>

**Distractor analysis** goes one level deeper on multiple-choice items: each wrong
option should attract *someone*, and should attract *weaker* students — a distractor
that pulls strong students usually signals an ambiguous stem or a defensible
"wrong" answer.

<Callout type="note" title="This is the screening IRT can't replace">
Latent models will happily fit broken items and hide the damage in strange parameter
estimates. Classical screening is how you notice — which is why it comes first in the
[IRT walkthrough](/wiki/quantitative/irt-fundamentals), and why jMetrik (a free GUI)
is a perfectly respectable way to do all of this before you're fluent in R.
</Callout>
