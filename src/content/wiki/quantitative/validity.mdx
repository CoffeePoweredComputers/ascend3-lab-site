Reliability asks *is the score consistent?* Validity asks the harder question: *does
the score support the interpretation we want to make?* This lesson traces how the
field's answer evolved, because all three eras still appear in papers — and reviewers
increasingly expect the modern one.

Messick unified the types into one construct-centered view <Cite id="messick-1995" />;
the **Standards** operationalized it as five *sources of evidence*
<Cite id="aera-apa-ncme-2014" />; Kane framed validation as building and testing an
explicit **interpretation/use argument** <Cite id="kane-2013" />. The best
short introduction written for education researchers in a discipline like ours:
Reeves & Marbach-Ad <Cite id="reeves-marbach-ad-2016" />.

## The five sources, applied to the DCI

1. **Content** — do the 20 items cover the debugging-ability blueprint, with nothing
   construct-irrelevant? Evidence: expert panel review against the construct map;
   the qualitative module's interview-grounded misconceptions as the item basis.
2. **Response processes** — are students *doing debugging* when they answer, or
   test-gaming? Evidence: think-alouds while solving items (the same skill the
   [qualitative module](/wiki/qualitative/overview) teaches).
3. **Internal structure** — do the items behave as one construct, reliably?
   Evidence: the [item analysis](/wiki/quantitative/reliability-and-item-analysis),
   dimensionality checks, [IRT calibration](/wiki/quantitative/irt-fundamentals), and
   eventually [DIF](/wiki/quantitative/dif-and-fairness).
4. **Relations to other variables** — do DCI scores correlate with things debugging
   ability should touch (course performance, time-to-fix) and not with things it
   shouldn't (reading speed)?
5. **Consequences** — what happens when the score is used? If instructors placed
   students by DCI score, whose interests would errors serve?

<Callout type="note" title="Where the running example is honest about itself">
The shipped pilot supplies evidence for source 3 only. A real DCI paper needs sources
1 and 2 before the psychometrics mean anything — which is exactly the staged plan the
[IRT walkthrough](/wiki/quantitative/irt-fundamentals) ends with, and why "we computed
α" is never a validity argument by itself.
</Callout>

## Kane in one paragraph

State the interpretation as a chain of claims — *scoring* (responses were scored
correctly) → *generalization* (these 20 items stand in for the domain) →
*extrapolation* (test behavior reflects real debugging) → *use* (decisions based on
scores are warranted) — then collect evidence for the weakest link first. The chain
makes explicit what "validated" hand-waves: every arrow is a place the argument can
fail, and different uses stress different arrows <Cite id="kane-2013" />.

No GenAI toolbox here, deliberately: assembling a validity argument is exactly the
judgment an LLM's fluent overclaiming would counterfeit. Write this one yourself.
