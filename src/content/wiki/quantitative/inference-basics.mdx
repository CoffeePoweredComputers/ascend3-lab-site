Everything inferential rests on one move: treating your data as a **sample** from some
larger **population** you actually care about, and quantifying how much your summary
would wobble across samples you didn't draw.

## Sampling variability, standard errors, confidence intervals

Run the SPRINT study again with 40 *different* students and every number changes a
little. The **standard error** is the size of that wobble; a **95% confidence
interval** is the range of population values compatible with your data. The CI is the
most honest single thing you can report: it carries the estimate *and* its precision.

<Reveal summary="What does '95%' actually mean?">
It's a statement about the procedure, not this one interval: across repeated studies,
95% of intervals built this way would contain the true value. Any single interval
either does or doesn't — you never find out which.
</Reveal>

**Who is the population, though?** In CER it's usually "students like these, in courses
like this" — because 86% of CER studies use convenience samples <Cite id="randolph-etal-2008" />.
That's fine *if the write-up says so*: claims scale with sampling, and "CS 2 students at
one university who chose this section" is not "novice programmers."

## The logic of a p-value

Null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) answers one narrow question: *if there were
truly no effect, how surprising would data like mine be?* The p-value is that
surprisal — the probability, **under the null**, of a result at least this extreme.

What a p-value is **not**:

- Not the probability the null is true.
- Not the probability your result replicates.
- Not a measure of effect size — with a big sample, a trivial effect earns a tiny p.
- Not a verdict: p = .049 and p = .051 are the same evidence <Cite id="wasserstein-etal-2019" />.

<Callout type="note" title="Two ways to be wrong">
**Type I** — the null is true, you reject it anyway (false alarm, rate set by α = .05).
**Type II** — a real effect exists, your study misses it. The second one is the
under-discussed epidemic in classroom research.
</Callout>

## Power: the class-sized-study problem

**Power** is the probability of detecting an effect that's really there. It rises with
sample size and effect size — and typical CER effects in course-sized samples leave
studies badly underpowered. Detecting a medium effect (d = 0.5) at 80% power needs
~64 students *per group*; a small effect (d = 0.2) needs ~394 per group. A two-section
pilot cannot do that, and computing p-values for a study that couldn't have detected
the effect misleads more than it informs <Cite id="guerzhoy-2023" />.

What to do instead, in order of preference:

1. **Design bigger** — multiple sections, multiple semesters, multi-institution.
2. **Estimate, don't test** — report the effect size with its (wide) CI and say the
   study is precision-limited.
3. **Never** run ten underpowered tests and narrate the significant one — that's the
   garden of forking paths, and a significant result from an underpowered study is
   *more* likely to be exaggerated, not less.

<Reveal summary="Quick check: a colleague's 2×20-student study found d = 1.1, p = .01. Believe the 1.1?">
Be suspicious. With ~20 per group, only large effects can reach significance — so
conditional on p &lt; .05, the estimate is inflated (a "winner's curse"). The direction
may well be real; the magnitude is almost certainly overstated. Look for the CI: it
probably spans from "modest" to "implausibly huge."
</Reveal>
