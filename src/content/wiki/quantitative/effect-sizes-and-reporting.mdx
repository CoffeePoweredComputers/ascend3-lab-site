A p-value says an effect is probably not zero. An **effect size** says how big it is —
which is the thing anyone deciding whether to adopt SPRINT actually needs. Reviews of
ICER papers found effect sizes systematically missing <Cite id="sanders-etal-2019" />;
this lesson is the fix.

## The workhorses

| Effect size | For | Reading it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cohen's d** | two-group mean differences | difference in SD units |
| **r** | associations | −1 … 1 |
| **η² / partial η²** | ANOVA | share of variance |
| **Odds ratio** | binary outcomes | see the [logistic lesson](/wiki/quantitative/logistic-regression) |

Which d, which η², and how to convert between them: Lakens' primer is the standard
practical reference <Cite id="lakens-2013" />.

## What d = 0.5 actually looks like

The labels "small / medium / large" hide how much two groups still overlap. Drag d:

<EffectSizeExplorer />

Even at d = 0.8 — "large" — the distributions share about two-thirds of their area.
Group effects are claims about distributions, not about every student, and writing
them that way ("a typical SPRINT student outscored about 66% of control students")
communicates more honestly than a band label.

<Callout type="warning" title="Benchmarks are context-free; your field isn't">
Cohen offered .2/.5/.8 reluctantly, for psychology. In education interventions,
d = 0.2 on a broad achievement measure can be a big deal. Compare against effects in
*your* literature, not against the generic bands.
</Callout>

## The reporting checklist

For every inferential claim, report: **the statistic, df, exact p, the effect size,
and its 95% CI** — plus the n behind it <Cite id="appelbaum-etal-2018" />. In one line:

> Welch's t(36.8) = 2.41, p = .021, d = 0.74, 95% CI [0.12, 1.36], n = 21/19.

And the study-level facts CER papers most often omit <Cite id="heckman-etal-2022" />:
research questions stated up front, who the participants were and how sampled, the
design (comparison group? assignment?), and a limitations section that names threats
honestly <Cite id="sanders-vahrenhold-mccartney-2023" />.

## The CER pitfall checklist

Documented, not hypothetical — check your draft against each:

- **No comparison group** — the modal published design was one-group posttest-only
  <Cite id="randolph-etal-2008" />.
- **Anecdote as evidence** — "students liked it" is a quote, not a result.
- **Missing effect sizes** — significance without magnitude <Cite id="sanders-etal-2019" />.
- **Forking paths** — many analyses, one reported. Pre-specify or disclose all.
- **Multiple comparisons** — ten tests at α = .05 ≈ 40% chance of a false positive;
  correct, or better, ask fewer questions.
- **Likert means without thought** — see [GLMs](/wiki/quantitative/glms-beyond-binary)
  <Cite id="kaptein-etal-2010" />.
- **Nesting ignored** — students inside sections aren't independent; see
  [clustered data](/wiki/quantitative/clustered-data-mlm-gee).
- **Unvalidated homegrown instruments** — see the entire
  [measurement block](/wiki/quantitative/measurement-foundations).

<Reveal summary="Practice: what's wrong with 'SPRINT works (p < .05)'?">
Almost everything reportable is missing: no effect size (how much?), no CI (how
precisely?), no test statistic or df (what analysis?), no n (what could it have
detected?), and "works" is causal language a two-intact-section design can't carry.
"The SPRINT section improved 1.5 points more than control (Welch's t(36.8) = 2.41,
p = .021, d = 0.74, 95% CI [0.12, 1.36])" says what happened.
</Reveal>
