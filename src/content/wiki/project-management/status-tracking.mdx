Status tracking gets a bad reputation because it is usually imposed — a burdensome report someone
else asked for. Flip that. The point of tracking is to make *your* work legible to *you*: what is
in flight, what is stuck, and what a "how's it going?" actually answers. Done lightly and owned by
you, it takes minutes a week and makes every advisor meeting sharper. The evidence from labs that
adopt agile-style tracking is consistent on one condition — it helps *when the student runs it*,
and becomes overhead the moment it is someone else's compliance form.

Three small habits do the job. None should take more than fifteen minutes.

## 1. A board with a limit

Keep a simple board — **Backlog → Doing → Done** — and put a hard **WIP limit** on the *Doing*
column: **two or three tasks, maximum.** The limit is the whole trick. Research tempts you to have
ten things half-started; capping work-in-progress forces you to *finish* before you *start*, which
is how things actually reach Done.

Each task needs a **Definition of Done** — the concrete condition that ends it — so "analysis" does
not linger as a task forever:

<Callout type="tip" title="Definition of Done, by task type">
- *Coding task:* "script regenerates Figure 2 from the raw CSV in one run."
- *Writing task:* "methods section drafted and shared with advisor."
- *Recruiting task:* "12 consented participants scheduled."

If you cannot state the "done" condition, the task is too vague to start — break it down first.
</Callout>

On the sheet, this is **Tab 3, Section A**: a Status dropdown (Backlog / Doing / Done / Blocked)
that color-codes each row, a Definition-of-Done column, and a Blocker column. The WIP counter turns
red when more than three tasks are *Doing* — a visible nudge to finish before you pull more.

## 2. A fifteen-minute weekly log

Once a week — pick a fixed time; Friday afternoon is a natural one — write four lines:

<Steps>
1. **Tried** — what you actually did this week.
2. **Learned** — what you now know that you didn't (including "this approach doesn't work").
3. **Decided** — any choice you made, so it is not re-litigated later.
4. **Next** — the two or three things that move to *Doing* next week.
</Steps>

Four lines a week becomes your project's memory. Six weeks in, it is the honest record of where the
time went — invaluable when a timeline needs re-planning, and half of your methods section already
written.

## 3. A 1:1 you drive

Walk into your advisor meeting with the agenda already written, in three parts: **Did / Blockers /
Next.** *Did* since we last met, *Blockers* you need help clearing, *Next* you plan to do. This
does two things: it puts you in charge of the meeting, and it aims the scarce time at the blockers,
where an advisor's input is worth the most.

<Callout type="note" title="Close the loop within a day">
After the meeting, send a **3–6 bullet decision summary** within ~24 hours — what was decided, who
owns each next action, and by when. It takes two minutes, prevents the "wait, what did we agree?"
drift, and doubles as the *Decided* line in next week's log. This lives in **Tab 3, Section B**:
one row per week/meeting with Did, Blockers, Next actions (owner + due), and advisor decisions.
</Callout>

If you want more structure, wrap the whole thing in **sprints** — fixed 2–6 week cycles with a
short plan-at-the-start and a review-and-retro at the end, where the retro asks not "did we finish"
but "how should we work differently next cycle." That cadence is the
[Scrum](https://scrumguides.org) idea adapted to a lab; the weekly log and board are the lightweight
version of the same loop.

## When the work is shared

The moment more than one person touches the same data or analysis, tracking has to cover
*coordination*, not just your own tasks — who is coding which transcripts, when you reconcile,
and by when. That last one changes character when other people are involved: your collaborators
have their own courses, deadlines, and lives, and a date you set *for* them is a date they never
agreed to. Shared deadlines are **arrived at together, not announced**.

<Callout type="note" title="Leaders speak last">
- **Don't** open the conversation by pushing for your preferred (usually early) deadline — you
  will get reluctant nods, not commitment.
- **Do** start by asking what their next few weeks look like: what they're carrying, what their
  plans are, and what a reasonable timeline would be *from where they sit*.
- Then speak last. Put your own constraint on the table (the conference deadline, the internal journal deadline) only
  after everyone else has laid out theirs, and agree on the dates together — they go on
  [your timeline](/wiki/project-management/timeline) with names attached.
</Callout>

already covers them:

## Where this lives

**Tab 3 · Status Tracking** of the companion sheet holds the board and the weekly/1:1 log. Keep the
header's *Last updated* current — the norm from [the overview](/wiki/project-management/overview) is
that **you update the tracker before the meeting**, so the 1:1 starts from a shared, current picture
instead of a reconstruction.
