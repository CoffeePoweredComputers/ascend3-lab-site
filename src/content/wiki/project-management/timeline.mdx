A research timeline is not a promise that the work will go as drawn. It is a *scaffold* — a shared
picture of what has to happen, in what order, that you revise on purpose as reality comes in. The
two mistakes are planning forward from today (which quietly assumes nothing goes wrong) and never
re-planning (which lets the dates rot until they are fiction). This lesson fixes both.

## Plan backward from the fixed end

Start at the date you cannot move — the conference deadline, the internal journal deadline, the
end of funding — and
walk **backward**, asking at each step *"what has to be true before this?"* Backward planning
surfaces the long-lead constraints that forward planning hides, and it forces the dependencies into
the open:

- You cannot analyze data you have not collected.
- You cannot collect data before **IRB approval** — a hard gate, often with a revision cycle.
- You cannot write the paper *at* the deadline; a strong draft has to exist weeks before it.

<Callout type="tip" title="The IRB gate is a real phase, not paperwork">
For human-subjects work, "IRB approved" is a milestone that blocks recruiting — and getting there
can take one or more revision rounds. Put it on the timeline explicitly, early, with its own
buffer. If you are unsure whether you need review or how long your path takes, work through the
IRB module first: [Do I Need IRB Review?](/wiki/irb/do-i-need-irb) and
[Find Your VT IRB Path](/wiki/irb/vt-irb-navigator), plus
[Writing a Protocol](/wiki/irb/writing-a-protocol) for what the submission itself involves.
</Callout>

## Milestones vs. deadlines

Every item on your timeline is one of two things, and confusing them is how schedules lie to you. A
**milestone** is a state of completion *you control* ("the analysis pipeline reproduces Figure 2").
A **deadline** is an external, fixed date *you don't* ("abstracts due March 1"). You plan around
deadlines; you make progress through milestones.

## Buffer every phase — on purpose

Research is nonlinear: recruitment stalls, IRB comes back with edits, an analysis reveals you need
more data. So every major phase gets a **named buffer** — plan on the order of **3–4 weeks** per
big phase, more for anything involving other people or approvals. A buffer is not padding you hide;
it is a line item you name, so when it gets used you *see* it get used. On the sheet, the timeline
has a dedicated **Buffer (wks)** column for exactly this.

## Draw it NSF-style

Funding agencies read a lot of timelines, so their conventions are a good default. An NSF-style
project timeline is a color-coded Gantt chart that a reviewer can parse in seconds:

<Steps>
1. **Rows are tasks, grouped by aim.** Sub-tasks sit under each aim (Aim 1: Setup & IRB, Aim 2: Data collection, Aim 3: Analysis, then Writing & submission), so structure mirrors the proposal.
2. **Time runs horizontally at a consistent resolution.** Quarters (Q1–Q4) under merged Year headers for a multi-year project; months for a ~2-year one. Bars span the active periods.
3. **Keep every time column the same width.** An 8-month task and a 24-month task must look proportional — unequal columns are the classic tell of a timeline nobody will trust.
4. **Mark milestones and decisions with glyphs.** A diamond (◆) for a milestone or deliverable, a triangle (▲) for a go/no-go decision point where the project could change course.
5. **Give it a color legend.** One fill color per aim/phase, in a boxed legend beside the grid, so the colors mean something.
</Steps>

<Callout type="note" title="The legend, in words">
On **Tab 2** of the companion sheet: blue = Aim 1 (setup / IRB), green = Aim 2 (data collection),
orange = Aim 3 (analysis), gray = writing & submission. ◆ = milestone / deliverable, ▲ = go/no-go
decision, a bordered cell = a named buffer. The tab ships with a **filled-in example** at the top
and an **identical blank template** below it — plan yours in the blank one.
</Callout>

## Re-plan on a cadence, not by drift

Here is the habit that separates a timeline that helps from one that embarrasses you: **plan the
near term in detail and the far term coarsely, then re-plan on a fixed schedule** as the unknowns
resolve. This is "rolling-wave" planning — you are not pretending to know month 18 in month 1; you
are committing to *revisit* the plan on purpose.

- Pick a cadence — end of each month, or each sprint — and put the re-plan on the calendar.
- At each re-plan, move dates deliberately and **write down what changed and why** in a **Re-Plan
  Log** (date · what moved · why). Silent slippage is how a project loses three months without
  anyone deciding to; a logged change is a decision you can defend.

<Callout type="warning" title="A moved date is a decision, not a failure">
Re-planning is the system working, not the plan breaking. The failure mode is a timeline that
still shows dates everyone privately knows are wrong. Update it, log the reason, and bring the
change to your next 1:1 — which is what the [status tracker](/wiki/project-management/status-tracking)
is for.
</Callout>
