Most of what derails a research project is not the statistics or the theory — it is drift: a
question that was never pinned down, a timeline that existed only in someone's head, a "how's it
going?" that neither person can answer with evidence. This module is about the small set of
**living documents** that keep a project honest, and the one habit that makes them work: **you
own them.**

Owning them doesn't mean knowing the answers. Nobody expects an undergraduate or an early grad
student to scope a study perfectly on the first pass — what you're asked to do is *try*: write
the first draft, bring it to the meeting, make the updates afterward. Your advisor is there to
catch what you can't see yet; that is what the meetings are for. But the plan, the timeline, and
the tracker stay in your hands, because a draft you wrote — wrong guesses and all — teaches you
more than a perfect one someone handed you, and a document someone else maintains is a document
you stop reading. Ownership is a skill you grow into. These pages, and the sheet that goes with
them, are where you practice it.

<Callout type="note" title="Who owns what">
A quick way to hold this in your head (borrowed from the RACI model project managers use):

- **You are Responsible and the Owner** — you create and update the brief, the timeline, and the tracker.
- **Your advisor is Consulted and Accountable** — they weigh in, catch problems, and answer for the work to the lab and funders.
- The rule that follows: **you update the document *before* the meeting, not during it.** The meeting is for decisions, not data entry.
</Callout>

## The four moves

The module is one workflow — **read → scope → schedule → track** — with a lesson per move:

1. **Map the literature** — a repeatable reading loop that turns your idea into a defensible gap.
   ([Find the Literature](/wiki/project-management/find-the-literature))
2. **A one-paragraph plan** — a structured abstract you write *before* the work, so the study is
   judged on its design and value, not on whether the results come out "positive."
   ([Scope It](/wiki/project-management/scope))
3. **An evolving, NSF-style timeline** — milestones and buffers you plan backward from the
   deadline and re-plan on a fixed cadence.
   ([Build & Re-Plan a Timeline](/wiki/project-management/timeline))
4. **A status tracker you run yourself** — a small board and a weekly log that turn "how's it
   going?" into a two-minute answer.
   ([Track It Yourself](/wiki/project-management/status-tracking))

The last three each live on a tab of the companion sheet; the reading lists from the first live
wherever you take notes — what matters is that you keep them.

The wiki is where the *reasoning* lives; the sheet is a lightweight instrument you fill in. Keep
the deep thinking here and the day-to-day state there.

## How to move through this module

Read it in order the first time — **read → scope → schedule → track** — because each move feeds
the next: the reading produces the gap, the gap anchors your one-paragraph plan, the plan names
the analysis that shapes the timeline, and the timeline sets what the tracker watches. After
that, treat the lessons as reference. You will come back to *Find the Literature* and *Scope It*
every time you start something new, and to *Build & Re-Plan a Timeline* every time reality makes
your old dates a fiction — which it will.
